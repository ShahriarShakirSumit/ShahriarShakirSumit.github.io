Shahriar Shakir Sumit

PhD Candidate in Computer Vision
School of Engineering and Technology, UNSW Canberra, Australia

I have been working in computer vision since 2019, and one question has stayed with me the whole way: why does a model that looks excellent on a benchmark stop working the moment the real world gets difficult? I have chased that question through object detection in poor lighting and heavy occlusion, transformer detectors, federated learning, and lightweight models built to run in real time on small devices.

More recently I have carried it into large language models, vision-language models and world models. Across all of these projects I keep finding the same lesson: models rarely fail at random. They fail under particular conditions, often for reasons a single accuracy number hides. I enjoy finding those conditions, explaining them, and building methods and tests that make AI systems more reliable where it matters.

I am in the final year of my PhD, supervised by Dr Murat Tahtali and Prof. Seyedali Mirjalili, and I will submit my thesis in December 2026.

What I work on

Detection in hard conditions

Finding people and objects under occlusion, low light and changing weather. Here, a detector learns to attend to the visible part of a half-hidden pedestrian (WACV 2027).

Small, fast and adaptive

Lightweight networks built with architecture search, distillation and pruning, and a training-free way to keep a frozen detector accurate when the scene changes, at under 1% extra cost per frame (ACML 2026).

Does the model really see?

Blur the evidence, then blur a matched decoy region. If the answer only changes for the evidence, the model was looking in the right place (ACML 2026).

Trustworthy decisions

Should a model answer or ask for clarification when the image is ambiguous? AAA-Bench tests exactly that (WACV 2027). I also study evaluation pitfalls in federated learning and world models.
